1. WHAT WE STARTED WITH & WHAT WE CREATED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Script Used : Models/Less Data/Data/preprocess_for_training.py
Saved Rules : Models/Less Data/Data/preprocessing_artifacts.joblib (Saved rules)

The Two Input Files (Clean data with real medical units like mg/dL and grams):
  1. Training File (2007-2016) : BioNutriGPT_train_2007_2016_clean.csv (23,694 people, 69 cols)
  2. Test File (2017-2020)     : BioNutriGPT_test_2017_2020_clean.csv  ( 7,046 people, 69 cols)

The Two Output Files (100% Numeric & scaled, ready to train the AI model):
  1. Training Ready (2007-2016): BioNutriGPT_train_2007_2016_ml_ready.csv (23,694 people, 93 cols)
  2. Test Ready (2017-2020)    : BioNutriGPT_test_2017_2020_ml_ready.csv  ( 7,046 people, 93 cols)

Why We Preprocessed Them Separately (Preventing "Data Leakage"):
  - The Golden Rule in AI: The model must never peek at future test data during training.
  - What we did: We calculated all averages, middle numbers (medians), and scaling rules
    ONLY from the older Training patients (2007-2016). Then, we applied those exact saved
    rules to both files separately.
  - Why this matters:
    1. Zero cheating: Future patients from 2017-2020 never influenced training rules.
    2. Same ruler: A scaled number like +1.5 means the exact same blood sugar or calorie
       level in both files.
    3. Ready for real hospitals: If a single new patient walks into a clinic today, we
       can transform their numbers instantly using these saved rules.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. STEP-BY-STEP PREPROCESSING (WHAT WE DID & WHY WE DID IT)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Step 1 - Kept IDs and Disease Labels Untouched (5 columns)
  - What: Kept Patient ID (SEQN) and Survey Year (NHANES_CYCLE) as reference tags.
    Kept the 3 disease labels (Diabetes, Obesity, Heart Disease) as clean Yes (1) or No (0).
  - Why:
    1. If we feed "Survey Year" to the AI, it might memorize the year instead of learning biology.
    2. Disease labels must remain pure 1s and 0s so the model can learn classification cleanly.

Step 2 - Added "Missing Question" Flags Before Filling Blanks (5 binary 0/1 columns)
  - What: Before filling in any blank answer, we added a 1 if the patient skipped the question,
    and a 0 if they answered it. We did this for Income Ratio (INDFMPIR_MISS), Sleep, Sitting,
    and Activity.
  - Why:
    In surveys, skipping a question is often a clue in itself (called "informative missingness").
    For example, people who skip income questions often have different financial situations
    than those who answer. These flags let the AI know someone skipped the question even
    after we fill the blank in Step 3.
  - How the Model Uses These 0s and 1s:
    Think of the flag like a light switch:
      * Flag = 0 (Answered): The model looks 100% at the patient's real answer. The flag stays silent.
      * Flag = 1 (Skipped): In Step 3, we fill the empty cell with a typical middle value (like 2.09).
        The "1" flag warns the model: "This number was filled in by us. Treat this patient based on
        how people who skip this question usually behave."
    This allows neural networks and decision trees to adjust their predictions without getting
    fooled by filled-in numbers.

Step 3 - Filled Remaining Blanks with Training Medians & Modes (No Guessing on Blood Labs)
  - What:
    * For numbers (like income ratio or sleep hours), we filled blanks using the middle number
      (median) from the Training set (e.g., Sleep = 7.0 hours, Income Ratio = 2.09).
    * For categories (like marital status or smoking), we filled blanks with the most common
      answer (mode) from Training (e.g., Non-smoker, Married).
  - Why:
    1. Why median instead of average? A few people exercise 20 hours a day, which pulls the
       average up too high. The median gives a true "typical" person.
    2. Note: All 4 blood tests and all 39 food nutrients were already 100% measured and complete,
       so zero blood tests or diet numbers were ever guessed.

Step 4 - Created 10 Clinical Ratios (Feature Engineering)
  - What: Calculated 10 standard medical ratios from each patient's own numbers:
    1. Cholesterol-to-HDL Ratio & Non-HDL-to-HDL Ratio: Heart disease risk markers.
    2. Protein, Carb, and Fat % of Calories: Shows diet quality instead of just total food eaten.
    3. Fiber per 1,000 Calories: Measures nutrient density regardless of meal size.
    4. Sugar-to-Fiber Ratio & Sodium-to-Potassium Ratio: Key markers for diabetes and blood pressure.
    5. Saturated-to-Unsaturated Fat Ratio: Measures bad fats vs. healthy fats.
    6. Sleep Deviation (|Sleep - 7.5 hours|): Measures how far someone sleeps from the healthy 7.5-hour sweet spot.
  - Why:
    AI models find it hard to do complex divisions on their own. By giving them established medical
    formulas upfront, we help the model spot disease risks much more easily.

Step 5 - Tamed Extreme Numbers with Log-Transform (log1p on 55 columns)
  - What: For numbers that have huge spikes (like Vitamin K, which ranges from 0 up to 15,000 mcg,
    or exercise minutes), we applied a standard mathematical curve called "log(1 + x)".
  - Why:
    If one person eats a huge amount of a vitamin, that giant number can confuse the AI's math,
    overshadowing other patients. Log-transform shrinks giant numbers into a manageable scale
    while keeping the order intact (higher intake is still higher, but without extreme spikes).

Step 6 - Clipped Rare Extreme Outliers (Winsorization at 0.1% and 99.9%)
  - What: Capped the top 0.1% and bottom 0.1% of extreme values based on Training thresholds.
  - Why:
    We used very gentle limits (0.1% and 99.9%) so we would NOT accidentally flatten real medical
    emergencies (like a severe diabetic with an HbA1c of 14%), while still preventing crazy
    computer typos from crashing model training.

Step 7 - Scaled All Numbers to Standard Score (StandardScaler: Mean = 0, Std = 1)
  - What: Converted all 64 continuous measurements so that 0 represents the average, +1 means
    above average, and -1 means below average.
  - Why:
    Without scaling, a calorie count of 3,000 would overpower an HbA1c blood test of 6.5 simply
    because 3,000 is a bigger number. Standardizing puts every single lab and food metric on an
    equal playing field.

Step 8 - Converted Categories to 1s and 0s (One-Hot Encoding)
  - What:
    * Yes/No questions (like sleep trouble or exercise) were turned into simple 1 (Yes) or 0 (No).
    * Multi-choice groups without a ranking (like Race, Marital Status, and Smoking) were split
      into separate 1/0 columns (for example: RACE_NH_WHITE = 1 or 0).
    * Education level (DMDEDUC2: 1 to 5) was kept as an ordered number (Grade School up to College).
  - Why:
    Computers cannot understand words like "Married" or "Divorced". But if you label them 1, 2, 3,
    the model might falsely assume "3 is greater than 1". Splitting them into separate 1/0 columns
    keeps the math unbiased.
