Calibrated Risk Assessment for Perimenopause and Practical Clinical Triage 
Utilizing Explainable Multimodal Learning 
 
Mohammed Tauqeer Ullah1*          Edara Sreenivasa Reddy2

Abstract: Perimenopausal risk assessment benefits from models that integrate metabolic, reproductive, and mental 
health signals into a single estimate that is reliable enough for threshold-based referral. Many screening pipelines still 
process laboratory markers and questionnaire-driven symptoms separately, which limits cross-signal interactions and 
can yield poorly calibrated probabilities. Standard baselines such as TabNet, FT -Transformer, SAINT, and TabR 
improve discrimination, but they provide limited support for  actionable, ranked intervention suggestions. This work 
presents MenoXplain, an explainable multimodal framework that applies modality -aware tabular attention for three -
class risk stratification and Top -3 intervention ranking, supported by SHapley Additive  exPlanations (SHAP) for 
global attributions and attention roll-up for instance-level traces. Experiments use the Centers for Disease Control and 
Prevention (CDC) National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017–March 2020 (Pre-Pandemic) 
subset of perimenopausal women aged 40–55 years (3,842 participants) with Demographics, Examination, Laboratory, 
Reproductive Health Questionnaire (RHQ), and Depression Questionnaire (DPQ) features. MenoXplain reaches 0.821 
accuracy, 0.793 Macro-F1, and 0.907 Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC), exceeding 
TabR (0.815, 0.788, 0.903) and improving calibration (Brier 0.142, expected calibration error 0.033). The framework 
supports clinic-facing triage and targeted follow-up, with limitations from cross-sectional data and offline evaluation 
of recommendation impact. 
Keywords: Perimenopause, Multimodal tabular learning, Health risk stratification, Intervention recommendation, 
Calibration, Explainable artificial intelligence, NHANES. 
 
 
1. Introduction 
Perimenopause is a midlife transition in which 
endocrine changes coincide with shifts in 
cardiometabolic status, sleep, mood, and daily health 
behaviors. Clinical risk rarely follows a single marker, 
and practical screening depends on combining 
measured vitals, laboratory biomarkers, reproductive 
health history, and patient -reported outcomes into a 
coherent decision view [1]. 
Personalized risk stratification in this setting is 
challenging because the evidence is heterogeneous 
and incomplete. Examination var iables are typically 
available, while laboratory values may be missing for 
many participants and questionnaire responses can 
vary in quality. A useful model must learn from 
partial observations without producing unstable 
predictions that undermine referral  thresholds. In 
addition, decision support cannot stop at risk 
prediction [2]. Screening systems are expected to 
suggest plausible next steps, such as lifestyle 
adjustment, mental health follow -up, or targeted 
clinical testing, and these suggestions need t raceable 
justification to avoid increasing clinician workload. 
Conventional screening often relies on rule-based 
scores or single -domain indices, which are 
interpretable but limited in capturing interactions 
across modalities [3]. Classical machine learnin g 
models improve flexibility, yet they can struggle with 
nonlinear relationships spread across feature groups 
and may output probabilities that are poorly

2. Related works 
Recent multimodal clinical prediction studies 
show that combining heterogeneous inputs can 
improve discrimination beyond traditional statistical 
baselines, but pract ical deployment barriers remain. 
Choi et al. [7] demonstrated real -time deterioration 
prediction in the emergency department using 
heterogeneous signals and reported better precision--
recall behavior than logistic regression, although 
AUROC and AUPRC reporting was not consistently 
available and the evidence remained tied to a single -
center retrospective setting. A broader synthesis by 
Krasniqi et al. [8] suggests that AUROC values above 
0.75 are frequently reported for multimodal 
neoadjuvant response prediction in breast cancer, yet 
direct comparison is weakened by differences in 
cohorts, endpoints, and evaluation protocols. Several 
task-specific systems report strong internal 
discrimination, for example PE -MVCNet for 
pulmonary embolism prediction reached AU ROC 
0.941 with accuracy 0.902 and F1 score 0.906 [9], 
while multimodal transformer modeling for 
postoperative delirium reported improved sensitivity 
and Youden index [10], but both examples highlight 
common concerns around preprint status, incomplete 
metric disclosure, and limited cross -site validation. 
Text and structured record fusion has also shown 
measurable benefit, as heart failure mortality 
prediction using admission notes with tabular 
features achieved AUROC 0.838 internally, 0.849 in 
prospective testing, and 0.767 externally [11], which 
illustrates both the value of multimodal integration 
and the typical performance drop under external 
generalization. Related screening and intensive care 
work reports gains from multimodal designs, but 
results can de pend heavily on cohort composition, 
supplementary-only reporting, and preprocessing 
quality for free -text and structured electronic health 
record features [12, 13]. Recent alignment and fusion 
preprints further emphasize calibration -aware 
objectives, contr astive alignment, and unified 
representations, often reporting improvements in 
AUROC, F1 score, or Brier score over gradient -
boosted baselines [14 -17], and additional directions 
include large language model based summarization 
for mortality prediction and electrocardiogram plus 
clinical-variable fusion for laboratory abnormality 
estimation with AUROC above 0.85 [18]. Across this 
body of work, two issues recur: probability 
calibration and decision -oriented outputs are 
frequently underreported, and model comparisons are

used to construct a perimenopause -focused cohort, 
learn multimodal representations from heterogeneous 
clinical feature groups, and generates both calibrated 
risk probabilities and ranked intervention 
recommendations, as shown in Fig. 1. The 
methodology consists of cohort construction and 
feature grouping, modality-preserving representation 
learning, modality -aware fusion, risk and 
recommendation heads, and a unified training 
protocol that keeps comparisons fair through a shared 
in-house implementation and identical data splits for 
all models.
